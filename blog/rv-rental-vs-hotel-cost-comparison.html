"Is an RV actually cheaper than a hotel?" is the question we get more than any other. The honest answer is: sometimes — and it depends almost entirely on how long you're going and how many people you're bringing. Anyone who tells you an RV is always cheaper is selling something. So instead of hand-waving, let's run a real trip with real numbers and show you exactly where the line is.

The Real Question

A motorhome isn't just a place to sleep — it's your lodging, your transportation, and your kitchen rolled into one vehicle. That's the key to the whole comparison. When you price a hotel trip, you're really pricing three separate things: rooms, a way to get around, and meals. An RV bundles all three. Whether that bundle saves money comes down to how many nights and people you're spreading it across.

Our Test Trip: 4 Nights, Family of Four

Let's use a realistic, common trip: a family of four (two adults, two kids) taking a 4-night trip from the Bay Area to Yosemite and back — roughly 400 miles round trip. We'll price each option the way a careful family actually would, and we'll keep the estimates honest. Your real numbers will vary with season, dates, and how you travel, so treat these as a worked example, not a quote.

A note on these numbers

Hotel rates, fuel prices, and campground fees swing a lot by season and availability — especially near national parks in summer. The figures below are mid-range 2026 estimates for illustration. Plug in your own real quotes before deciding; the structure of the comparison matters more than any single number.

Option A: Hotel + Rental Car

For a family of four near Yosemite, you're typically looking at one hotel room (or two, if your kids are older and you want space). Gateway-town lodging near the park runs high in season. Add a vehicle if you flew in, parking, and — the big one — three restaurant meals a day for four people.

Where the Break-Even Point Is

The single biggest factor is trip length, because the RV's fixed costs (the rental itself) get spread thinner each night while the hotel's per-night room cost never drops. Here's the rough pattern for a family of four:

Trip lengthWho usually wins
1–2 nightsHotel — the RV's rental and prep costs don't have enough nights to pay off
3 nightsAbout even — this is the typical break-even point
4–7 nightsRV — cooking savings and one bundled vehicle pull ahead
7+ nightsRV, clearly — the longer you go, the wider the gap

Group size pushes the line the same direction. The moment a hotel trip needs a second room, the RV — which sleeps four to six in one vehicle — usually jumps ahead even on shorter trips.

What the Numbers Don't Capture

Cost is only half the decision. A few things never show up on a spreadsheet but matter just as much:

  • You wake up in the park. No hour-long morning drive in from a gateway town — you're already at the trailhead at sunrise when the crowds are thin.
  • One "room" for the whole family. Nobody's split across two hotel rooms; kids and parents are together, which younger families especially appreciate.
  • Your kitchen comes with you. Picky eaters, dietary needs, early-morning coffee, late-night snacks — all handled, no hunting for an open restaurant.
  • Flexibility. Love a spot? Stay another night. Weather turns? Move on. You're not locked into a non-refundable reservation.
  • The trip is part of the fun. For a lot of families the motorhome is the vacation, not just transport to it.

When a Hotel Is the Better Call

We rent motorhomes, but we'd rather you book the right trip than the wrong one. A hotel genuinely makes more sense when:

  • It's a quick one- or two-night getaway — not enough nights for the RV math to work.
  • You're traveling solo or as a couple and won't fill a bigger space.
  • You want to be in the heart of a city where parking a 25-foot motorhome is a headache.
  • You have zero interest in cooking and dining out is the point of the trip.

If that's your trip, a hotel is the smart money. If you're going longer, bringing the family, and heading somewhere with nature around it — that's exactly where the RV pulls ahead.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is renting an RV actually cheaper than a hotel?

It depends on trip length and group size. For a one- or two-night solo trip, a hotel is usually cheaper once you account for the RV's nightly rate, fuel, and campground fees. For a family of four on a trip of four nights or longer, an RV often comes out ahead because it bundles lodging, transportation, and a kitchen into one cost — and you avoid booking multiple hotel rooms. Our worked example below puts the break-even point around three nights for a family.

What hidden costs come with renting an RV?

The main extras beyond the nightly rate are fuel (Class C motorhomes average roughly 8–10 mpg), campground or RV-park fees (commonly $35–$80 a night), a one-time cleaning or prep fee, and optional add-ons like a generator or Starlink. Mileage may be capped with a per-mile charge over the limit. At Crystal RV we include insurance, 100+ items of gear, and a full orientation, so there is no surprise equipment-rental line.

What hidden costs come with a hotel trip?

Hotel trips add up beyond the room rate: a rental car or gas and parking, resort or parking fees, and especially restaurant meals for every person three times a day. For a family, dining out is often the single largest cost of a hotel-based trip — frequently more than the rooms themselves. National-park lodging also books out months ahead and carries premium rates.

How many people can sleep in a Class C RV?

Crystal RV's Class C motorhomes sleep four to six people comfortably, depending on the floor plan, using a combination of an over-cab bunk, a rear bed, and a convertible dinette. That means one rental covers a whole family that would otherwise need two hotel rooms.

When does a hotel make more sense than an RV?

A hotel is usually the better value for short one- or two-night trips, solo or couple travel where you are not splitting a bigger space, city-center stays where parking a motorhome is hard, or trips where you will not cook and want to be in the heart of downtown. The RV wins on longer trips, larger groups, nature-focused destinations, and any trip where you value cooking your own meals and waking up at the trailhead.

Run Your Own Numbers

The takeaway: for short solo trips, book the hotel. For a family heading out for three nights or more — especially somewhere like Yosemite, Tahoe, or the coast — a Class C RV usually costs about the same or less and gives you a fundamentally better trip. Price both honestly for your dates and the right answer is usually obvious.
