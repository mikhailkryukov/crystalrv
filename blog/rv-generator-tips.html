RV Generator Tips: Power Your Trip Safely and Efficiently

What a generator actually powers, how to run it safely, the carbon-monoxide rules that matter most, and when you don't even need it. A practical guide for renters.

A generator is what lets an RV run real appliances when you're not plugged into shore power — the difference between roughing it and having your air conditioning, microwave, and outlets work in the middle of nowhere. For renters it's simple to use and only needs a few safety rules respected. Here's the practical version.

What an RV Generator Powers

The generator produces household-style power for the things your batteries alone can't run:

  • Air conditioning — the big one; AC needs the generator (or shore power) to run
  • Microwave and higher-draw kitchen appliances
  • Wall outlets for laptops, hair dryers, and chargers
  • Recharging the house batteries while it runs

Things like your lights, water pump, fridge (on gas), and furnace fan typically run off the house batteries and don't require the generator at all.

Do You Even Need It?

Often, not much. If you're staying at campgrounds with shore power hookups, you plug in and the generator stays off — the pedestal does the work. You mainly need the generator when you're boondocking (camping without hookups), running the AC on a hot day off-grid, or using a high-draw appliance away from a plug. Knowing this saves fuel and noise: don't run it out of habit.

Safety First: Carbon Monoxide

The rule that matters most

A generator produces carbon monoxide, which is odorless and dangerous. Only run it outdoors with the exhaust pointed away from the RV, never in an enclosed space, and never while parked close to another RV's windows. Keep your RV's windows and vents on the exhaust side closed, and make sure the CO detector is working. When in doubt, don't run it while you sleep.

  • Never run a generator in a garage, near an open window you're sleeping by, or in any enclosed area
  • Keep the exhaust side clear and pointed away from people and other campers
  • Confirm the carbon-monoxide detector is functioning — it's there for exactly this
  • Let it cool before refueling, and don't refuel while it's running

How to Run It

  1. Make sure you have adequate fuel (most RV generators draw from the main fuel tank, often with a reserve cutoff so you can't strand yourself)
  2. Turn off high-draw appliances before starting, then start the generator
  3. Let it run for a minute to stabilize before switching on the AC or microwave
  4. Turn appliances off before you shut the generator down
  5. Run it under a light load occasionally rather than fully idle, which keeps it healthy

Fuel & Efficiency

  • RV generators are fairly economical, but running the AC all day uses noticeable fuel — plan your tank accordingly
  • Many built-in generators stop automatically when the main tank drops to roughly a quarter, so you can't accidentally burn all your drive-home fuel
  • Only run it when you need the output; for charging phones and running lights, the house batteries are plenty
  • Shade, window coverings, and good ventilation cut how much AC — and therefore generator time — you need

Quiet Hours & Etiquette

Generators make noise, and campgrounds care about it. Most enforce quiet hours (commonly evening to morning) when generators must be off. Even outside those hours, be considerate — point exhaust away from neighbors, keep runs reasonable, and don't fire it up at dawn. National and state parks often have stricter generator rules or designated areas, so check the campground's posted policy.

Hookups & Battery Power

For many trips you'll barely touch the generator:

  • Shore power: at a hookup site, plug in and everything runs off the pedestal — no generator needed
  • House batteries: cover lights, water pump, fridge on gas, phone charging, and the furnace fan for a night or two off-grid
  • Starlink and devices: if you add Starlink, it sips power and runs off the system easily
  • Use the generator to top up batteries and run the AC or microwave when you're off-grid, then shut it off

Frequently Asked Questions

What does an RV generator power that the battery doesn't?

The generator powers high-draw items the house batteries can't sustain — mainly the air conditioner, microwave, and wall outlets — and it recharges the batteries while running. Lights, the water pump, the fridge on gas, and the furnace fan typically run off the batteries without the generator.

Is it safe to run an RV generator while sleeping?

It's safest not to. Generators produce odorless carbon monoxide, so if you must run one overnight for AC, ensure the exhaust points away from the RV, windows and vents on that side are closed, and a working CO detector is in place. When in doubt, run it before bed to cool the RV, then shut it off to sleep.

Do I need the generator if I'm staying at campgrounds?

Usually not. At sites with shore-power hookups you plug into the pedestal and everything runs without the generator. You mainly need it when boondocking without hookups, running the AC off-grid, or using a high-draw appliance away from a plug.

Will the generator drain my fuel?

It uses fuel, and running the AC all day is the heaviest draw, but RV generators are fairly economical for normal use. Most built-in units draw from the main tank and shut off automatically when it drops to about a quarter, so you can't accidentally use the fuel you need to drive home.

Are there rules about running a generator at campgrounds?

Yes. Most campgrounds enforce quiet hours when generators must be off, and national and state parks often have stricter limits or designated generator areas. Always check the posted policy, point exhaust away from neighbors, and keep runs considerate.
